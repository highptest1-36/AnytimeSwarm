\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The held-out study separates three effects that are often studied together. The first is whether to search at all. Under
uncertain execution, every ALNS variant served more reward than regret-2 repair in every configuration of the primary
matrix, so the benefit of search does not vanish when the plan is executed with errors. The second is how much to
search. A long fixed budget lost reward through late plans, and a short fixed budget lost reward on large fleets. The
adaptive stopping rules avoided both losses without tuning per scale, and AnytimeSwarm-R did so with the least
computation. The third is when to replan. Periodic replanning raised the utility of every method by more than the choice
between the stopping rules did. For a practitioner, this ordering suggests to choose the trigger first and the stopping
rule second.

The execution-aware delay cost did not deliver the gain that motivated it. Its pooled advantage over the strongest
baselines was a few tenths of a percentage point, and only the comparison with the budget of 40\,ms excluded zero before
correction. Two explanations are plausible. First, the delay cost only changes the moment at which the search stops.
The predicted improvement usually falls quickly, so the deterministic and the risk-aware costs stop the search at
similar points, and AnytimeSwarm-R stopped on average \val{JMsRedAS}\% earlier than AnytimeSwarm. Second, the remaining
finish-time error is corrected by the next replanning call, which follows at the next event or, with the periodic
trigger, within 60\,s. A risk-aware cost may matter more when replanning is rare or expensive, or when the error model
enters the plan itself, for example through stochastic or chance-constrained
routing~\cite{gendreau1996stochastic,ponda2012chance}, instead of only the decision to stop. The reduction of
computation is the more robust benefit. It matters when the planner runs on embedded hardware or shares the processor
with perception and control.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. Tier C uses three UAVs and fifteen tasks per mission, so it validates the
decision problem at a small scale and with a limited number of missions. Software-in-the-loop simulation reproduces the
autopilot and the vehicle dynamics but not the sensors, the radio link or the weather of a field test. Wind is constant
during a mission, and the finish-time error model is linear in the planning horizon and fitted per disturbance level, so
the level itself is assumed to be known. The latency factor emulates a slower planning computer instead of measuring
one. All experiments ran on a single laptop, and the measured computation depends on it. Finally, the methods share one
ALNS and one family of scenarios, and other search methods or mission types may show different gains from adaptive
stopping.
