\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The three studies suggest an order of priorities for a multi-UAV replanner under uncertain execution. The planning
model matters most. Moving every deadline by the predicted finish-time error raised the utility by more than any choice
of stopping rule or trigger, and it removed the losses from late plans. The trigger comes second. Periodic replanning
helped every method on the nominal model, but only by \val{KDabsAblTrig}\,pp on the chance-constrained model, which
suggests that both correct the same accumulated deviations, one before and one after they occur. The stopping rule
comes last. On the proposed model, it was equivalent to a matched fixed budget, and its value lies in avoiding the
losses of long budgets on slow planners. The metareasoning rules behaved in the same way, probably because the
improvement curve of this ALNS flattens within a few tens of milliseconds. Monitoring rules may matter more for searches
whose improvement continues for longer.

The proposed model and the constant buffer both shift deadlines forward, and the buffer was the strongest baseline. The
difference lies in the shape of the margin. A constant margin is too large for tasks reached soon and too small for
tasks at the end of a route, whereas the proposed margin grows with the lead time. A hard chance constraint, however,
discards every task whose probability of being served on time falls below $\Phi(z)$, even when a vehicle has nothing
better to do. Study~3 confirmed this explanation on new seeds. Keeping the given-up tasks with their nominal deadline
removed the weakness and left the guarantee of the other tasks intact, but many fallback tasks were served late. A soft
formulation that weights every task by its probability of being served on time would make this trade explicit and could
be tuned to the cost of a late arrival in a given application.

The two tiers play different roles. Tier B provides the number of missions that a paired comparison needs, and tier C
checks that its conclusions survive an autopilot, a physics engine and a real middleware. In both studies with flights,
tier B predicted the utility of individual flights closely, and tier C reproduced the mechanism but could not resolve
differences of a few percentage points. A two-tier study should therefore plan its number of flights from the smallest
difference that it is meant to confirm.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The error model is linear in the lead time and fitted per disturbance
level, so the level is assumed to be known, and $z$ was selected per level on development missions. The chance
constraint is imposed on every task separately rather than jointly on a route. The fallback was designed after the
held-out results of study~2 and was tested on new seeds, but only in tier B. Tier C uses three UAVs and fifteen tasks
per mission, and many of its runs ran slower than intended because the laptop was shared with other work.
Software-in-the-loop simulation reproduces the autopilot and the vehicle dynamics but not the sensors, the radio link or
the weather of a field test, and the wind is constant during a mission. The latency factor emulates a slower planning
computer instead of measuring one. Because the stopping rules read a wall clock, repeated runs are not bit-identical, and
the mean utility of a method varied by up to \val{JRepeatDiffMax}\,pp between two runs of the same missions. Finally,
all methods share one ALNS and one family of scenarios, and other search methods or mission types may change the size of
the gains.
