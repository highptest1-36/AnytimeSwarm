\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The two studies give an order of priorities for a multi-UAV replanner under uncertain execution. The planning model
matters most. Moving every deadline by the predicted finish-time error raised the utility by more than any choice of
stopping rule or trigger, and it removed the losses from late plans. The trigger comes second. On the nominal model of
study~1, periodic replanning raised the utility of every method by more than the stopping rule did, but on the
chance-constrained model its contribution fell to \val{KDabsAblTrig}\,pp, which suggests that both correct the same
accumulated deviations, one before and one after they occur. The stopping rule comes last. It guards against long budgets
on slow planners, where the full budget of 250\,ms served \val{KDabsFix}\,pp less reward than the proposed replanner
and \val{KDFixKTwoHundred}\,pp less at $\kappa_B=200$, and it
finds a suitable amount of computation on every fleet scale without tuning. On the proposed model, however, it was
equivalent to a fixed budget of 40\,ms that had been matched to its mean computation on development data.

The proposed model and the constant buffer both shift deadlines forward, and the buffer was the strongest baseline.
The difference lies in the shape of the margin. The error grows with the lead time, so a constant margin is too large for
tasks that are reached soon and too small for tasks at the end of a route. The proposed model scales the margin with the
lead time and served more reward than the buffer in \val{KWinBuf} of the \val{KNConfigs} configurations. Under high
disturbances with halved windows, the order reversed. A chance constraint discards every task whose probability of
being served on time falls below $\Phi(z)$, even when the vehicle has nothing better to do. With short windows and large
errors, many tasks fall below this threshold. An objective that weights each task by its probability of being served
on time, instead of a hard constraint, would keep such tasks as fallback options and may remove this weakness.

The stopping rules from the metareasoning literature behaved like the learned rule. On the nominal model, the
performance profile, online performance prediction, the learned predictor, the size-scaled budget and ALNS with
40\,ms all lay within \val{KUSpreadStop}\,pp of each other, although they used between \val{KMsOnl}\,ms and
\val{KMsAS}\,ms per call. The improvement curve of the ALNS used here flattens within a few tens of milliseconds, so
every reasonable rule stops near the same point and the differences in reward stay small.
Monitoring rules may matter more for search methods whose improvement continues for longer, or on slower hardware. The
execution-aware delay cost of study~1 did not deliver the gain that motivated it either. It changed only the moment at
which the search stops, and the remaining error was corrected at the next replanning call. Letting the error model shape
the plan, as the proposed model does, was far more effective than letting it shape the decision to stop.

The two tiers play different roles. Tier B provides the number of missions that a paired comparison of replanners
needs, and tier C checks that its conclusions survive an autopilot, a physics engine and a real middleware. In study~1,
tier B predicted the utility of individual flights closely and reproduced the two robust findings, the loss of the long
fixed budget and the lower computation of AnytimeSwarm-R. It did not predict the order of methods whose utilities differ
by less than one percentage point, and neither tier can resolve such differences with the number of missions used here.
The per-mission difference between two methods in tier C had a standard deviation of \val{JCSdDiff}\,pp, so confirming a
difference of half a percentage point with a power of 80\% would require about \val{JCNPower} missions per method, far
beyond what a software-in-the-loop campaign of one night allows. A two-tier study should therefore state its effect
sizes before running and plan the number of tier C missions from the smallest difference that it is meant to confirm.
Study~2 repeated this pattern. Tier C reproduced the mechanism of the proposed model, fewer late and no skipped
tasks, but not the utility gain, whose tier B estimate on the same missions was itself not significant. With a
per-mission standard deviation of \val{KCSdForty}\,pp, about \val{KCNPowerForty} missions per method would be needed.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The finish-time error model is linear in the lead time and fitted per
disturbance level, so the level itself is assumed to be known, and the confidence parameter $z$ was selected per level on
development missions. The chance constraint is imposed on every task separately rather than jointly on a route. Tier C
uses three UAVs and fifteen tasks per mission, so it validates the decision problem at a small scale and with a limited
number of missions. Software-in-the-loop simulation reproduces the autopilot and the vehicle dynamics but not the
sensors, the radio link or the weather of a field test, and wind is constant during a mission. The latency factor
emulates a slower planning computer instead of measuring one. All experiments ran on a single laptop, and the measured
computation depends on it. Because the stopping rules read a wall clock, repeated runs are not bit-identical, and the
mean utility of a method varied by up to \val{JRepeatDiffMax}\,pp between two runs of the same missions. Finally, all
methods share one ALNS and one family of scenarios, and other search methods or mission types may change the size of the
gains.
